# Quest — Research

Skin: Questcapades (`questcapades.json`) · Domain: Tabletop Roleplay — Systems, Stories, and the Tables That Tell Them · Meter: fun
Coverage: 82 topics, 112 poles/positions, 12 archetypes (all counts taken from the skin JSON).

## 1. Domain framing

Quest maps tabletop roleplaying: the seat a player takes, the systems and worlds they love, the dice-and-danger customs they keep, how stories and characters are built, how combat and challenge run, the norms and logistics of the table itself, and the fandom around the hobby. Quest is an **action** skin, not a stance skin: its topics are practices — things people do together at a table — so a position here is a rated practice, not a belief. On a multi-pole topic, a position is advocacy for one pole of the practice (how the game should be run and played); on a single-pole topic it is affinity for the one named practice, where a negative rating is aversion to doing it; on a directional (give/receive) topic the practice is rated on two flanks — running it and playing in it (or, for Hosting & snacks, hosting and being hosted) — and direction is never averaged, because running a horror game and playing in one are genuinely different experiences. What does not count as a position is mere familiarity: knowing a system is not a position, wanting it at your table is. The meter word is **fun**: the skin measures how strongly a profile is into each pole or practice (advocacy, per pole) and how open it is to each topic at all (openness, per topic) — in this skin's terms, how much of the fun lives where. Two widget rulings apply throughout: xy-switchbox topics (Rulings vs. Rules, Tone, Shared Worldbuilding, Acting vs. Third-Person Narration, Grid vs. Theater of the Mind, Challenge Style, Metagaming & Table Talk, and Spotlight & Party Cohesion) cross two independent yes/no axes that are not opposites — the status quo is both axes negative — and multi-pole poles are independent positions that need not be opposites of one another. Receptivity and intensity are not rated directly — receptivity is derived as round-half-even(openness \u00d7 advocacy) computed with exact fractions, and intensity is the signed largest-|value| pole, derived from the advocacy values. The Scribe is an indaba: it uses this research to show each practice with its backing, name where tables meet and part, and ask what would let the group reach consensus — in this skin, the session-zero agenda a table can agree before the first roll. The skin's stance is table-pluralist: no play style is treated as the correct one; old-school lethality, collaborative storytelling, tactical crunch, and solo journaling are placed on one map in one vocabulary so a group can find its splits before they become fights.

## 2. Topic rationale


### Your seat at the table

**Your seat** — Your seat is the first fact of any table: who runs, who plays, and whether the chair rotates decides almost everything downstream. The Game master pole: You build the world, voice its people, and hold its rules. The Player pole: You bring one character and live in what the table builds. The Rotating pole: The chair moves: this arc yours, the next one mine.
**Player styles** — Player styles name the character stance a player brings to every party — anchor, wanderer, or gap-filler — which parties quietly plan around. The The Anchor pole: You always take the same solid role in the party — the healer, the tank, the face — and the party plans around it. The The Wanderer pole: You experiment: a different role, a different kind of character, every new game. The The Gap-Filler pole: You read the party first and take whatever role is missing, so the group works.
**Game mastering** — Game mastering as a practice in itself: affinity for running games, the hobby's scarcest resource and its oldest bottleneck. It is a single-pole affinity topic: one practice, rated for strength, with negative values meaning aversion.
**Playing a character** — Playing a character is the complementary practice — affinity for being a player, full stop, which some lifelong GMs never get to exercise. It is a single-pole affinity topic: one practice, rated for strength, with negative values meaning aversion.

### 3.1 Your seat at the table


#### Your seat
- **Game master** — Reasons: running the game is the creative seat: world, cast, and rules in one pair of hands; the GM's fun is real fun — building a world other people get to live in is the hobby's deepest craft Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: playing in a long campaign that gave them the player's joy so fully they wanted the other seat as often.
- **Player** — Reasons: the player's seat is the purest play: one character, fully inhabited, with the world as a genuine surprise; bringing one character well is a discipline the GM's divided attention never permits Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: running a one-shot that turned out to be more fun than a year of playing.
- **Rotating** — Reasons: rotation keeps both crafts alive and shares the hobby's scarcest labour; a table where the chair moves never burns out its one GM, and every player learns what running costs Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a rotating table whose campaigns kept dying at every handover.

#### Player styles
- **The Anchor** — Reasons: the anchor is the party's load-bearing member: a known healer, tank, or face lets everyone else plan, specialize, and take risks; reliability is a gift a player gives the whole table Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a campaign where the anchor role became a cage the player could not leave.
- **The Wanderer** — Reasons: the wanderer treats each game as a new instrument to learn — a different role is a different game inside the game; novelty is how this player stays in love with the hobby Statistic: No reliable statistic found. ### 3.6 Dice, death & danger


#### Dice honesty
- **Fudge to save the story** — Reasons: the story is the table's shared treasure and a single roll can be a thief: fudging, sparingly and invisibly, protects months of build-up from one meaningless crit; the screen exists for a reason Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: learning that a beloved campaign moment was fudged, and feeling the whole history of the table cheapen.
- **Fudge only to avert disaster** — Reasons: honest dice with one quiet exception is most experienced GMs' actual practice: the thumb comes out only for the roll that would end the campaign *wrong*; the discipline is in how rarely it happens Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: discovering the exception had quietly become the rule.
- **Let them fall** — Reasons: let them fall is the trust position: dice that can be overridden are decoration, and stakes that can be negotiated are not stakes; the table that trusts its dice gets real fear and real triumph, which no fudge can manufacture Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a campaign killed by an honest roll so pointless that the table itself voted to rewind it.

#### Random character generation
- **Random character generation** — Reasons: random generation discovers characters no one would have designed: the dice hand you a one-armed shepherd with genius-level luck and the roleplay writes itself; rolled characters also equalize — nobody's concept outranks the table's fortune Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a randomly generated character so mismatched to its player that the mismatch, not the game, became the campaign.

#### Lifepath character creation
- **Lifepath character creation** — Reasons: lifepath creation front-loads the biography: by the first session the character has a childhood, a career, and enemies, all rolled honestly; Traveller's famous risk — dying in character creation — is the genre's badge of honour Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a lifepath so rich the actual campaign felt like an epilogue to it.

#### Point-buy building
- **Point-buy building** — Reasons: point-buy is fairness formalized: the same purse for every player means builds differ by choice, not by dice luck at creation; it also teaches the system — spending points is the first real game decision Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a point-buy table where the fairness of the purse produced five identical optimal purchases.

#### Character death
- **Character death** — Reasons: death on the table is what makes courage a real choice: a game where characters can die is a game where their risks mean something; tables that love lethal games love them for the honesty, not the body count Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a beloved character's death so random it converted the whole table against lethality.

#### Total party kills
- **Total party kills** — Reasons: the TPK is the campaign's true fail state, and its possibility is what makes a party's survival an achievement rather than a schedule; affinity for TPK-permitting tables is affinity for endings that are earned in both directions Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a card-based system whose shuffling and dealing overhead ate the pace it was bought for.

#### Character Mortality
- **Plot-Armored Arcs** — Reasons: plot armour protects the investment: a character with forty sessions of arc should not die to a corridor trap; the story is the asset, and random death is a tax on exactly the players who invested most Statistic: No reliable statistic found. - **Telegraphed Fair Lethality** — Reasons: telegraphed lethality is the fairness settlement: death is real, but it knocks first — warnings, omens, and escalating danger make every death a choice the player made with open eyes; stakes without cheap shots Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a 'telegraphed' death the whole table agreed had not, in fact, been telegraphed.
- **OSR Meat-Grinder Danger** — Reasons: the meat-grinder is the old school's honesty: fragile characters, real dread, and cleverness as the only armour — OSR tables prize the fear because it makes caution, planning, and retreat into gameplay Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a meat-grinder dungeon whose body count stopped being frightening and started being funny.

#### Loot and treasure
- **The point** — Reasons: loot is the point is the founding contract: the delve is a treasure hunt, the score is kept in gold, and 'check the barrel' is a complete philosophy of play; gold-as-score made the original game a game Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: an incidental-loot table that discovered, too late, its game's whole advancement economy ran on treasure.

#### Heists

### 3.13 The table itself — Table norms & safety


#### Session zero
- **Session zero** — Reasons: session zero is the table's constitution: tone, boundaries, and expectations agreed before they are needed; the skin's own framing is right — the splits over dice, death, and tone are cheaper found here than after the first fight Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a session zero so thorough it became the campaign's most memorable session, which is its own kind of warning.

#### Safety tools — lines & veils
- **Safety tools — lines & veils** — Reasons: lines and veils are consent made practical: agreed in advance, usable in the moment without a speech; they let tables play closer to difficult material, safely, because the exits are marked — the hobby invented this layer itself, at tables, and it works Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a table whose safety tools existed on paper and were quietly mocked whenever anyone reached for one.

#### Phones at the table
- **Phones at the table** — Reasons: phones at the table are the modern rival for attention: tables that welcome them gain character sheets, dice rollers, and quick lookups; the phone is a tool the hobby already uses, and the demonization is generational habit Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a phone-welcome table that lost a climactic reveal to a group chat about lunch.

#### Metagaming & Table Talk
- **Strict In-Character Fog** — Reasons: strict fog is immersion's discipline: playing only what your character knows manufactures real suspense and real discovery; the fog is what makes information a treasure inside the fiction Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a strict-fog table whose players began keeping secrets from each other off-table too.
- **Open Writers'-Room Collaboration** — Reasons: the writers' room is collaboration at full strength: dramatic irony shared openly lets every player aim their character at the best story, not just their own survival; the table conspires, kindly, against its characters Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a writers'-room table whose openness dissolved a mystery the GM had spent a season building.

### 3.14 The table itself — Logistics & hosting


#### Hosting & snacks
- **Hosting & snacks** — Reasons: hosting is the table's hidden labour and its hospitality: a room, a meal, and a host who makes five people welcome weekly; being hosted well is its own pleasure — and noticing the labour is the guest's part of the practice Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a hosting arrangement whose invisible labour, once finally noticed, turned out to have been resented for a year.

#### Weekly game night
- **Weekly game night** — Reasons: the weekly night is the campaign's heartbeat: a fixed rhythm that survives busy lives because it asks no weekly negotiation; regularity compounds — worlds deepen when the table meets like a standing appointment with wonder Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a weekly game whose fixed night became an obligation its players feared to break more than they enjoyed keeping.

#### Scheduling the game
- **Scheduling the game** — Reasons: scheduling is unglamorous and decisive: the person who herds five calendars is doing the hobby's most consequential work; affinity for solving it — polls, reminders, ruthless kindness about the date — is affinity for games actually happening Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a scheduling regime so optimized it optimized the spontaneity, and then the group, away.

#### Teaching new players
- **Teaching new players** — Reasons: teaching is how the hobby reproduces: every veteran was once the newcomer someone patiently explained advantage to; tables that love teaching stay young, and the hobby's growth is measured in explained dice Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a teaching table whose veterans' patience curdled into a hierarchy the newcomers could feel.

#### Spotlight & Party Cohesion
- **Unified Cooperative Party** — Reasons: a unified party is momentum: characters who pull together let the campaign spend its hours on the world instead of on itself; trust inside the fiction is what long campaigns are built from Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a unified party so harmonious it had no internal story left to tell.
- **Inter-Party Friction & Secrets** — Reasons: friction and secrets are drama's fuel: divergent loyalties, honestly played between aligned players, produce the scenes tables retell for years; the axis is real because the risk is real — it works exactly when the players, unlike the characters, are on the same side Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a secrets-heavy party whose friction escaped the fiction and lodged between the players.

### 3.15 The table itself — Play aids & atmosphere


#### Playing on a VTT
- **Playing on a VTT** — Reasons: the VTT saved the dispersed table: friends across three time zones still play weekly, with maps, tokens, and automation no kitchen table matches; online play is not the hobby's fallback, it is its second home Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a VTT campaign whose automation quietly played more of the game than its players did.

#### Miniatures & terrain
- **Miniatures & terrain** — Reasons: miniatures make the fiction tangible: a painted figure *is* your character in a way a token never is, and terrain turns a fight into a place; the craft wing of the hobby is play before the session even starts Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a terrain collection so magnificent its owner could no longer bring himself to play anywhere else.

#### Maps & handouts
- **Maps & handouts** — Reasons: maps and handouts are props with evidentiary force: the letter you can hold, the portrait you can study — physical artefacts anchor the shared imagination, and a good handout is a session's souvenir and its clue in one Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a handout so dense it required its own session to read.

#### Music & ambience
- **Music & ambience** — Reasons: music is the cheapest immersion there is: the right playlist turns a kitchen table into a tavern in four bars; ambience is the GM's invisible set design, and tables that curate it feel the difference in their first minute Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: an ambience setup whose boss-fight track looped so noticeably it became the session's running joke.

### 3.16 Formats & fandom


#### Actual-play podcasts
- **Actual-play podcasts** — Reasons: actual-play podcasts are the hobby's broadcast wing: they teach the game by showing it, recruit players by the thousand, and turned table culture into a shared canon; listening to a great table is its own evening's entertainment Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a beloved actual-play whose professional polish quietly made a home table feel inadequate by comparison.

#### Watching streams
- **Watching streams** — Reasons: watching streams is the hobby live: chat, chaos, and a table performing for an audience that is also a community; the biggest streams are events, and their tables set styles the whole hobby borrows Statistic: Critical Role's Legend of Vox Machina Kickstarter raised $11.3 million in 2019, the hobby's clearest measure of actual-play's scale (Kickstarter, 2019). #### Session write-ups
- **Session write-ups** — Reasons: write-ups are the campaign's afterlife: the session becomes a story others can read, and the table becomes its own historian; the written record is also how absent players, and future nostalgia, are served Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a write-up practice so thorough it became a second, unpaid campaign for its author.

#### Campaign journals & wikis
- **Campaign journals & wikis** — Reasons: the campaign wiki is long memory made useful: names, debts, and prophecies a two-year campaign cannot hold in five heads; the Lore Keeper's archive is infrastructure, and infrastructure is a form of love Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a wiki so complete that consulting it mid-session became the session.

#### Publishing your own adventures
- **Publishing your own adventures** — Reasons: publishing is the hobby's authorship dream: your dungeon, in print, at strangers' tables; the ladder from handout to sold module is how the hobby's professional literature has always been recruited Statistic: No reliable statistic found. What would move a holder: a published adventure whose reviews taught its author what their home table had been too kind to say.

#### Dice & book collecting
- **Dice & book collecting** — Reasons: collecting is the hobby at rest: dice with heft, shelves with spines — the collection is a museum of tables past and a promise of tables future; the shelf is part of the pleasure, and pretending otherwise fools nobody Statistic: No reliable statistic found. ## 5. Gap analysis

**Positions with no archetype.** The map's practices are wider than its 12 archetypes. No archetype primarily embodies: the host (Hosting & snacks as an identity — the table's material labour has no persona); the scheduler-organizer (§3, Scheduling the game); the streamer or actual-play performer (§3, Watching streams; Actual-play podcasts); the collector (§3, Dice & book collecting); the designer-publisher (§3, Publishing your own adventures); or the simulationist worldbuilder (§3, Simulationist detail). The three individual samples — Maren (who runs the Friday table), Theo (a bard who talks first), and Priya (a min-maxer with spreadsheets) — are real-profile samples, not archetypes, and are therefore covered by name here rather than in §4; Maren embodies the organizer-GM the archetype list lacks, and Priya the Min-Maxer persona in individual form.

**Thin topics.** Sixty-three topics carry no poles at all: 38 are `single` affinity topics and 25 are directional `give_receive` topics. Per the skin's design these are valid: a single topic's one position is the practice itself (positive = affinity, negative = aversion), and a directional topic's position is the practice rated on its two flanks, which §3 treats as one position covering both directions, since direction is never averaged. They are thin in one specific way: the indaba can list backing for the practice but cannot locate a disagreement *within* the topic the way a multi-pole topic allows. The eight xy-switchbox topics are not thin and are not oppositions: each crosses two independent axes, and §3 gives each axis its own entry.

**Skin-JSON problems (reported, not fixed).** (1) Eighty topics have an empty `question` field. Twenty of them carry a hovertip `issue` that serves as the de facto question — those are quoted as recovered questions in §2 and collected in §5.1. The other sixty have no hovertip issue either (most have no hovertip at all), so no question can be recovered for them; §2 and §3 for those topics rest on the topic name, its type, and its give/receive labels where present. (2) Quest's topics largely lack the `widget` field (63 of 82), and pole-bearing topics mix trilinear-line, triangle, and xy-switchbox widgets; §3 follows the poles as listed regardless. (3) The samples Maren, Theo, and Priya have no `emblem` field in the JSON (emblem is null), unlike the 12 archetypes, which all carry emblem paths. (4) Two topics share near-duplicate territory — Character death (xqu0032, single) and Character Mortality (xqu0076, three poles), and Dice honesty (xqu0028) overlaps both — §3 covers each as listed without merging them.

**Deliberately left out of this research.** Specific commercial systems' sales rankings beyond the two statistics in §6; advice on which style a table *should* adopt, since the skin's stance is pluralist; and any statistic not listed in §6 — where §3 could not ground a number in a source named with confidence, it says so rather than estimating.
